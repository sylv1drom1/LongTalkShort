Most of us were never taught how to love someone well. We pick it up from films, from our parents' example, and from trial and error — usually more error than we'd like. So it's worth pausing when two people who have spent fifty years watching couples up close, in a lab wired with cameras and heart-rate monitors, tell us that much of what we believe about relationships is simply wrong.

That's the uncomfortable, freeing message running through John and Julie Gottman's conversation on *The Diary of a CEO*. Here's what stuck with me.

### The compatibility myth

Start with the belief that quietly runs most of modern dating: that somewhere out there is a person who matches you — same interests, same values, same background. The Gottmans call this flatly wrong.

There's a well-known study they point to in which women were asked to smell t-shirts worn by different men and pick the scent they liked best. The men they preferred weren't the ones most *like* them — they were the ones most genetically *different*, specifically in the genes governing the immune system. The evolutionary logic is that two divergent immune systems give a child broader protection. At a biological level, we're not drawn to our own reflection at all.

This matters because "we're not compatible" is one of the most common reasons people give for walking away — and it may be built on a false premise. Difference isn't the problem. Difference is often the point.

### There is no "the one"

The other great myth is the soulmate. The Gottmans have a deflating statistic for it: roughly 69% of the conflicts a couple has are *perpetual* — rooted in personality or lifestyle differences that never fully resolve. Every pairing, no matter how loving, comes with a permanent set of friction points. Whoever you choose will snore, or chew loudly, or leave the cupboard doors open, forever.

So the search for a flawless match is a setup for disappointment and, worse, for endless procrastination. In a country of hundreds of millions of people, John suggests, there might be hundreds of thousands you could build a wonderful life with. The realistic target isn't a perfect person — it's a "good enough" relationship with someone you trust, which you then build into something great over time. The cracks, as Julie puts it, can be part of the beauty.

### Why desperation repels — and confidence attracts

If there's one energy that undermines people looking for love, it's desperation. Not because being needy is a moral failing, but because of what it does to your attention. When you're desperate to be liked, you turn inward at the worst possible moment — anxiously monitoring the impression you're making instead of being genuinely curious about the person across from you.

And curiosity, in the Gottmans' view, is the whole game. Everyone is interesting when they're with someone interested in them; nobody is at their best with someone busy evaluating them. John describes the "evaluative context" — treating a date like a job interview — as the antidote to love.

Steven draws a sharp parallel from business: as an investor, he's far more likely to back a founder who makes him feel like an *option* than one who's demandingly desperate. Need signals weakness; sufficiency signals value. It's the same in dating. The person who's genuinely fine with or without you is the one who's free to be curious — and that's what's magnetic.

Can you fake it? Julie says no. People who fake confidence overshoot — they brag without realizing it, and it reads as hollow. She tells a disarming story about a boy in high school who boasted constantly, whom she couldn't stand — until she realized the intensity of her reaction came from recognizing the same insecure impulse in herself. Fake confidence is just insecurity in costume, and people feel the seam.

### Building real confidence

Both Gottmans grew up criticized — John describes being made to feel worthless from the ground up — so this isn't abstract for them. Their prescription has three parts.

First, do the internal work, often through therapy. So much dating misery comes from people hiding a shame-based self behind an idealized mask; when reality fails to match the mask, they feel rejected — when what's really being rejected is the mask itself.

Second, practice self-acceptance, sometimes literally in the mirror, repeating "this is who I am" until the internalized critical voice loses its grip.

Third — the most practical tool — confront distorted comparisons. Julie notes that a young woman may have seen millions of idealized, edited images by the time she's eighteen, giving her an impossible baseline. Her antidote is to seek out *reality*: look at real bodies in their genuine variety, and you start to see how manufactured the standard is.

The punchline undoes the whole appearance-and-status game: John says attraction and love aren't really about looks, or IQ, or education. They're about the heart — whether this is a kind, caring person who treats everyone, including the waitress, as a human being.

### Sex: connection, not novelty

When it comes to sex, the Gottmans challenge the popular idea that love and desire are opposites — that security kills the spark. Yes, novelty is arousing (they cite the "Coolidge effect," the pull of the new). But the biggest finding cuts the other way: a study of 70,000 people across 24 countries found that the real difference between great and poor sex lives came down to *affection and emotional connection*. Couples with great sex say "I love you" and mean it, kiss passionately for no reason, cuddle, and go on real dates.

Quantity, meanwhile, has no relationship to happiness — quality does. The one genuine problem is a mismatch, where one partner needs sex frequently and the other could take it or leave it. For many people, especially women, emotional safety isn't the enemy of desire; it's the prerequisite for it.

### The Four Horsemen: what predicts a breakup

Here's where the lab work becomes genuinely predictive. The Gottmans measured the ratio of positive to negative moments in couples' interactions. In stable, happy relationships, positives outnumbered negatives about five to one — even during conflict. In couples heading for divorce, that ratio collapsed to less than one to one.

Four specific negatives were the strongest predictors of a relationship's demise:

- **Criticism** — attacking your partner's character ("you're so lazy") instead of raising a specific issue.
- **Contempt** — the most corrosive of all, which Julie calls acid for a relationship: disgust, mockery, sarcasm, looking down on your partner.
- **Defensiveness** — meeting a complaint with victimhood or a counterattack instead of taking any responsibility.
- **Stonewalling** — shutting down completely, often because the person is physiologically "flooded" (heart rate racing past 100, thinking brain offline).

The hopeful part: these are patterns, and patterns can change once you know what to look for and practice the alternatives.

### The tools that actually help

The Gottmans don't just diagnose — they prescribe. **Talk for about 90 minutes a week** — but the magic isn't the time, it's listening *without defensiveness*. John admits he keeps a notebook to take notes when Julie says "we need to talk," reflecting back what he hears.

**Speak from "I feel… about the situation,"** not "you did this to me." The blame is what triggers the defensiveness. And hold a **"State of the Union" meeting**: start with five specific appreciations, then move to what needs to improve, and close with a question John loves — *how can I make you feel loved this week?*

Underneath all of it is gratitude — not as a nicety but as a habit of mind. Unhappy couples, one study found, notice only about half of the positive things their partners actually do. Happy couples see it all.

### Affairs are more treatable than you'd think

An affair — emotional or physical — always involves deception and broken trust, and it detonates the betrayed partner's entire sense of reality. The betrayed person often develops genuine PTSD, which is why the pain can't simply be apologized away.

And yet the Gottmans report a recovery rate of around 75% using a three-stage model — always in therapy, never DIY:

- **Atone** — the partner who strayed answers every question with total transparency (though not the graphic sexual details, which only fuel traumatic flashbacks) and apologizes, sincerely and repeatedly.
- **Attune** — the couple examines what was wrong in the relationship itself. Affairs are usually driven by *loneliness* and a habit of avoiding conflict, far more than by a hunger for sex.
- **Attach** — rebuilding trust and recommitting.

It's atonement, not forced forgiveness. And asked whether an affair can ever *help* a relationship, Julie says that when couples get real help, it very often does — because the crisis finally forces them to learn who the other person truly is.

### The smallest thing that matters most

If you take one idea away, make it this one. In their apartment lab, the Gottmans noticed that partners are constantly making tiny "bids for connection" — "look at that boat," a sigh, showing you a photo. The content is trivial; the real question underneath is always *are you there with me?*

You can respond three ways: turn toward the bid (acknowledge it — a nod, a "what is it?"), turn away (ignore it), or turn against it (snap irritably). Turning toward, John is careful to say, isn't about your body — it's about responding to your partner's reach.

Six years later, the couples who had stayed married had turned toward each other's bids 86% of the time. The couples who divorced had done so just 33% of the time. Not in the big crises — in the boat-and-photo moments, hundreds of times a day. Turning toward also quietly builds the shared humor that later defuses conflict, and it can be learned simply by becoming aware of it.

And when you genuinely can't respond? Don't turn away in silence. Put in a bookmark: *"I'd love to hear this — can we finish it in an hour?"* — and mean it, with warm eyes and a warm tone. Because, as John's partner reminds him, it's not the words. It's how you say them.
